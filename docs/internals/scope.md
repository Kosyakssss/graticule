# Scope, small

Scope has two halves: a recorder inside every app (`kernel::scope`) and a window of its own (`crates/scope`) that attaches to a running app. The small version records what each frame cost and why it was drawn, and shows Time and Why it redrew. Item 19 adds the asks: boxes, commands, facts, effects and jobs, taste, history and rewind, and overlays drawn in the app. Facts can be changed from Scope too.

## The recorder

The shell keeps a `Recorder` for the window. Each input is timed; when it asks for a frame, its cause is noted (the first cause since the last frame wins), and the time spent on input is added to the next frame's `update`. Each frame records a `Stamp`: its number, when it was drawn, why, and the time in microseconds spent on input, building the view, laying it out, painting, handing quads to the GPU, after the frame (effects, the input method, accessibility) and in all. The runner measures view, layout and paint itself and gives them to the shell through `Face::stages`; a face that doesn't measure them has its whole paint counted as painting.

A stamp goes into a fixed ring of 1024, preallocated, under an uncontended lock. While Scope is attached it is also offered to a bounded channel with `try_send`, which neither blocks nor allocates; if Scope falls behind, stamps are dropped rather than slowing the app. Measured in release on the Windows machine: 39 ns a frame, most of it the one clock read (28 ns) for the frame's time; the shell's six clock reads around the stages cost about as much again.

## How Scope finds an app

At launch the recorder listens on a port on 127.0.0.1, chosen by the system, and writes the app's title and the port to `Graticule/Scope/<process id>` in the user's data folder. The file is removed when the app closes normally; Scope removes any whose port no longer answers. Nothing listens beyond this machine, and nothing is sent until Scope connects. A thread waits for the connection, blocked, so idle costs nothing. It serves one Scope at a time.

## The wire

Text, one record a line, tab-separated: `launch <stage> <ms>` for each launch stage, then `frame` followed by the stamp's fields in order (`Stamp::line`, `Stamp::read`). On connecting, Scope gets the launch stages and the whole ring, oldest first; after that, each new frame as it is drawn. The connection's end is noticed at the next frame the app tries to send.

The wire runs both ways. A second thread reads what Scope writes, one ask a line (`Ask::line`, `Ask::read`): `ask boxes`, `ask commands` or `ask overlay <grid> <boxes>`. It hands each to the shell as an event, and the shell gives it to the face and draws a frame, recorded with the cause "Scope asking". The runner answers in that frame's paint, after the tree is laid out, so the answer describes what is on screen; the shell sends answers after the frame's stamp. An answer is a block of lines between `boxes begin` and `boxes done` (or `commands`): `box <index> <parent> <role> <col row cols rows> <file:line> <why> <text>` for every node, and `heading <text>` or `command <label> <keys> <why not>` in the order the help sheet uses. Text is cut to one line. When Scope lets go, the reading thread asks for no overlay, which draws a frame, which also ends the writing side; the app is back as it was without waiting for its next frame.

Why a box is its size is worked out when asked, not recorded during layout, so layout pays nothing for it: `Layout::explain` compares the box with what the code asked for (fixed columns, least and most), what its content wants (`size`, `rows`) and what its parent gave. It reads the laid-out tree, so it names the outcome and its nearest reason, not the whole chain of decisions.

Facts and effects are answered differently from boxes, because they grow with the data. Facts are copied (lists share their chunks, so the copy is cheap), turned into a value and written as lines on a thread of their own, which wakes the window when the answer is ready; the answer goes out after the next frame. The lines are `fact <depth> <name> <value>`, a list's items named by their place, cut at 5,000. A line holding a plain value (yes or no, a number, words, an option that holds nothing, or one of those inside an `Option`) ends with its path: the shape of its value, its kind of state and its steps, fields by index and list items by key, as in `b:d:0/#1/2`. The facts thread finds them from names with `Field::field_named` and `Field::item_keys`, since a fact's value leaves out live and resource fields and so can't be counted. "Change “done”…" in Scope asks for the new value in the palette and sends `ask set <path> <text>`; the app reads the text as the same shape of value (`scope::read_set`) and makes it an edit through its own `Edit` command, so it is one undo step named "Set from Scope to …", logged like any other, and then answers with its facts again. An app without an `Edit` command, a path that is gone, or text that isn't that shape of value is said in the app's message line and changes nothing. Values inside an enum's data have no path, since a path can't step into a variant. Effects come from a ring of the last 200 the runner started, kept as each starts and stamped when its job sends back or stops: `effect <kind> <what> <seconds ago> <ms taken> <outcome>`. Scope reads every answer on its reading thread too, so its own main thread only swaps in a finished list.

Taste laws that judge one laid-out tree live in `kernel::taste`, pure functions from a tree to the boxes that break them. Assay calls them over every state of every app and reports the first in each; the runner calls them when Scope asks, answering `flaw <law> <file:line> <said>`, and while the taste overlay is on, outlining each such box in the danger ink at twice the hairline. Assay depends on the kernel, so a law that the running app must also judge has to live below it.

Rewind is undo and redo, not a second mechanism. `ask history` answers with `applied <n>` and a `step <label>` for every step on the branch redo follows (`History::line`); `ask rewind <n>` makes the runner undo or redo one step at a time, each turning in time slices as a user's undo would, until `n` are applied, then answer `history` again. Going back past a change and making a new one starts a branch, exactly as undo does, so nothing Scope does to history is out of the user's reach.

The overlays are quads that belong to no node, kept in their own slot of the topmost kept layer (`Kept::loose`), so turning one on or off rewrites only those quads. The grid is the same corner marks Specimen draws; the outlines are a hairline in the link ink around every box with an area. While an overlay is on it is redrawn with every frame, a cost that grows with the boxes on screen, not with the data.

## Scope's window

Scope is an ordinary Graticule app (`Scope`) with a thin face around its runner that owns the connection, as Specimen's face owns its runners. A thread reads the lines and wakes the window, at most once until the window has caught up, so an app drawing a thousand frames a second doesn't wake Scope a thousand times. On waking, the frames arrive as one internal command; Scope keeps the last 600, newest first. Its views are Running apps, Time (the slowest 1% against the 1 ms budget, launch stage by stage, and every frame's stages with a bar in block characters, a tenth of the budget per cell) and Why it redrew (the causes tallied, then frame by frame).

## Not yet

The laws of §13.6 (with Scope off the frame time is the same; with Scope on the app stays within budget; Scope shows and subtracts its own cost) compare frame times with and without Scope, which only means something on the reference machine in release builds; they wait for it. Assay's budgets (`assay.md`) already judge the frames of every app. History over the wire comes with rewind. Source locations are recorded in every build: each node keeps the `Location` of the `ui` call that made it, which costs a pointer.
